The CRM behind a cash-on-delivery operation

Cash on delivery means anyone can type a phone number and cost you a courier trip. No card, no deposit, no signal — until the parcel comes back. This is the system we run instead, and the parts of it I got in the wrong order.

NestJS · API Next.js · web PostgreSQL + Redis 528 commits 113 suites · 1,274 tests passing

The problem

A COD operation carries risk that a card-payment business simply does not. The customer commits nothing at checkout. Every order is a promise, and the courier fee is spent before you learn whether the promise was real.

Three things were costing us money, and only one of them felt urgent:

  • Fake and duplicate orders — nothing stopped the same number ordering the same item four times in a minute.
  • Courier drift — three carriers, three dashboards, three CSV formats. Comparing them was a manual spreadsheet job nobody had time to do weekly.
  • No per-channel profit — "we had a good month" was a feeling, not a figure. Channel-level cost was not tracked against channel-level revenue.

Architecture

Fraud scoring

It is not AI. It is arithmetic someone can audit, which matters more when it decides whether a real customer's order ships. Fourteen signals, each carrying fixed points and a weight. Scores band into three outcomes.

Bands

0–30 green pass, no action · 31–60 yellow flagged for review · 61–100 red auto-hold, managers notified, dispatch blocked

The design decision that matters: weights load from a fraud_rules table, falling back to the in-code constants for anything an admin has not overridden. A threshold can be retuned during a bad week without a deploy, and every check writes to fraud_checks with the signals that fired — so a held order can always be explained to the customer who placed it.

The courier layer

Three carriers, one interface. Adapters self-register at boot into a registry, so nothing downstream imports a concrete carrier — the dispatch path asks for adapters that are enabled, cover the district, and can carry the parcel.

  • Steadfast, Pathao and Paperfly are registered and live.
  • The registry refuses to let two adapters silently claim the same id — the original comment in that file notes parcels would otherwise go to "whichever registered last — a coin flip in production."

An honest note: a fourth adapter, RedXAdapter, is written and implements the interface, but it is not imported or registered in couriers.module.ts. It is built, not wired. Three carriers are in production.

Reconciliation and per-channel profit

The two least glamorous modules, and the ones that changed the most.

COD reconciliation exists because the courier's figure and ours disagreed more often than anyone wanted to admit. When money moves through a third party and comes back days later, "we think we were paid" is not an accounting position.

Per-channel profit exists because channel revenue without channel cost tells you nothing about which channel to spend more on. A channel can carry the highest revenue and the worst margin at the same time, and until the two sit in the same view you cannot see it.

What I would do differently

I built fraud scoring before reconciliation.

Fraud was the loud problem. A held parcel is visible, someone complains, it feels like the thing to fix. Reconciliation was the quiet one — a variance nobody noticed in any single week, accumulating every single week.

Those were not the same size of problem, and I picked the one that felt urgent over the one that was expensive. If I started again, the ledger would come first and the scoring second.

The second thing: I shipped before I trained. The first version of adoption was "here is the login, it is better than the spreadsheet," which did exactly nothing. What worked was sitting next to people while they entered real orders — which is also how I found the two fields that were my problem to fix rather than theirs to learn.
